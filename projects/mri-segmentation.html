Finding a tumour with no labels

Brain-tumour segmentation on BraTS 2018 using nothing but clustering: k-means, and a Gaussian mixture model fit by an EM algorithm written from scratch in PyTorch and run on the GPU over nine million voxels at a time. On six of ten patients both hit a Dice score around 0.9. On the other four they showed exactly where unsupervised methods break — and one of those breaks needed a human in the loop to paper over.

k-means can only draw circles of equal size, so it cuts the long white-matter population in half and smears the tumour into whatever is nearest. The GMM fits an ellipse to each population and usually wins outright. Now drag the tumour down to a dozen voxels: both collapse. That's patient 7. Then raise K to 5 — the small cluster comes back, along with the question of which of five clusters is the tumour. That's the manual step in the pipeline.

Why unsupervised

BraTS 2018 provides 285 patients, each scanned in four MRI modalities — T1, contrast T1, T2 and FLAIR — at 240×240×155 voxels, with tumour regions outlined by neuroradiologists. The supervised route is well trodden. The question here was different: with no labels at all, how far do classical clustering methods get, and where exactly do they stop?

The framing is deliberate. Manual annotation is the expensive part of every medical imaging pipeline. A method that needs none of it is worth understanding even when it loses to one that does — especially if you know precisely which tumours it will miss.

The pipeline

Voxels into feature vectors

Each modality is z-scored to zero mean and unit variance, so contrast differences between scanners don't dominate. Then every voxel is replaced by the mean of its 3×3×3 neighbourhood — a light 3-D smoothing that cuts the noise which otherwise fragments clusters. The result is one 4-vector per voxel, (μT1, μT1c, μT2, μFLAIR), and 8.9 million of them per patient.

Cluster with K = 5

Five populations are assumed: background, white and grey matter together, CSF, the brain edge, and whole tumour. k-means is scikit-learn's. The Gaussian mixture is not — it is a hand-written EM in PyTorch with full 4×4 covariances per component, log-sum-exp in the E-step for stability, a Cholesky log-determinant, and a guard that reverts the parameters if the log-likelihood ever drops. It runs on the GPU because at nine million points per patient nothing else is practical, and it is seeded with the k-means centroids so that EM starts near a sensible optimum rather than a random one.

Pick the tumour out of five clusters

Clustering gives labels, not names. Three of the five were assigned by rule: the mode of the corner voxels is background; the largest remaining cluster is white and grey matter; the smallest is the brain edge. That leaves two — CSF and tumour — and they could not be told apart by count or by probability.

The step that isn't automated. CSF and tumour were separated by looking at them. The paper says so plainly, and it is the honest limit of the method: the pipeline is unsupervised right up to the final binary decision, where it needs a person. It is also the same problem the playground shows when K goes to 5.

Six patients — 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 9 — segment well by either method, with GMM ahead on most and its best case reaching Dice 0.929. The other four are the interesting ones, and they fail in three distinct ways.

Patients 3 and 10 have sensitivity above 0.87 and precision around 0.3: the tumour was found, but a lot of non-tumour came with it. That is over-segmentation, and the higher-K fix the paper proposes would address it directly.

Patient 8 is where the two methods part company. k-means scores 0.003 — it found nothing. GMM scores 0.348, which is poor, but it outlined the tumour where k-means drew a blank.

Patient 7 defeats both, and the reason is visible before any clustering runs. The tumour is tiny. With five clusters there is no fifth population for it to occupy; it is absorbed into whatever tissue surrounds it. Sensitivity 1.0 and precision 0.002 says it all — the "tumour" cluster is almost everything, so it technically contains the real one.

Where it stops

The paper closes with two limitations, and both are structural rather than incidental. The small-tumour failure follows from fixing K at five: a population that small can't claim its own component. Raising K would help, and the playground above shows it helping — but every extra cluster makes the last step, deciding which cluster is the tumour, harder. And that step was already manual.

Which is the more honest conclusion: unsupervised clustering gets a long way on large, well-contrasted tumours, and the point at which it needs a human is precisely the point at which the human's judgement is hardest to replace. The directions named for future work — dimensionality reduction to fit across patients jointly, and GAN-based unsupervised segmentation — both attack that boundary.
