My Daily News: an AI front page that can prove it showed me everything

Every morning it reads 48 public AI sources — company and research blogs, release feeds, changelogs, independent writers, and six standing rankings such as GitHub Trending, Hacker News and Hugging Face Daily Papers — keeps only what I have not seen, has an LLM summarise and categorise each item, and shows one page per category in my order. It is built to be read over breakfast, and to say out loud when a morning's page is incomplete.

Status: ongoing — a working prototype, now in a code-frozen measurement. The prototype has been used every morning since 24 September; from 28 September its code is frozen while five mornings of real reading are measured against thresholds written down before any data existed. The production rebuild and deployment have not started; the code is public on GitHub. Last updated 2026-10-04.

What it is

A personal front page for one field. The idea started as "a personalised Hacker News for everything I follow", with AI as the first domain; Hacker News named the idea, not the layout. The sentence the prototype had to satisfy on its first run is written into the specification, and it carries its own limit underneath:

"On any morning, everything my AI sources have published that I have not already been shown is split into my categories, one page each in the order I set; within each page items are sorted by my interest, every item carries a concise LLM summary, the layout is switchable — and I finish the pages I care about over breakfast."

"The reach is bounded, and the bound is the compromise: a feed is read seven days back, a trending list only as it stands now. A first run therefore shows a week rather than a day, and anything older than a source's window is never shown at all."

Sources. Forty-eight, of three kinds: 33 RSS and Atom feeds through one shared reader, nine page readers for sites that publish news without a usable feed, and six standing rankings. Every source is a checkbox in Settings, grouped by the organisation behind it — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA, Hugging Face, Mistral, DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, independent writers such as Simon Willison and Andrej Karpathy, and so on.

Each morning. I press Fetch. The sources are read concurrently, each with its own deadline, so one slow site cannot hold up the rest; items whose feed carries too little text to summarise have their page fetched once. Everything new is sent to the model, which writes a short summary, a clearer title where the source's own is just an identifier, and picks one of six categories. Rankings skip all of that and go straight to their own page. The page names each step as it runs; a normal morning takes about ten seconds.

The page. Three groups: News — six category pages in my order, Products & launches, Model releases, Tools & libraries, Business & policy, Writing & talks, and Research last, outside the breakfast read; Trending — one pane per ranking, as it stood at the fetch; and Others for anything that fits no category. Two layouts, a newspaper and a one-item-at-a-time stack. Any item can be saved to a collection that keeps its own copy. A calendar opens any past day exactly as it was captured.

The decision everything rests on: what the product looks at vs. what it shows

The obvious design is a time window: show what was published since the last fetch, or in the last 24 hours. Measured against real data, it loses news. On 24 September seven OpenAI posts arrived carrying publication times about 4 to 15 hours before the previous morning's fetch — which had not contained any of them. The feed made them visible long after the time it stamped on them. Against that day's items, a seven-day window kept all 24 dated items; a 24-hour window kept 8; "since the last fetch" kept 3; "since the start of yesterday" kept 17 and still lost all seven. Because the delay belongs to the source, no window anchored on publication time can be tightened without losing items.

So the two jobs are separated. A wide window — seven days, rolling back from the moment of the fetch — decides what the product looks at. A memory of every item already shown decides what counts as new. The window does not have to be right about when a source publishes, and the memory does not have to be right about time at all. On the day this was measured the sources served 115 items; 84 had been shown on an earlier day and were held back, and 31 reached the page.

The memory compares reading days, not moments. An item is held back only if it was first shown on an earlier day. Otherwise a second press of Fetch the same morning would return nothing, because everything it found would already be remembered — and since the page always shows the day's latest fetch, the morning's news would disappear. The reader's day starts at 06:00 local, not at midnight, so a late-night reading still belongs to the day it feels like.

What makes two items the same

An item is identified by its source, its publication time and its normalised title — three things the source decides and my code cannot. The URL is deliberately not the identity. Twice, a one-line change to how a source's link was read gave every item from that source a new identity, and the product served a whole week of it again as if it were new. Every link an item has ever arrived under is kept with it, and the link shown is always the one the source published, character for character.

A page that accounts for itself

The product's first promise is everything my sources published, and a reader cannot check that by looking at a page of summaries. So one line sits above the tabs every morning: "✓ 47 of 47 sources read · 1 disabled · 2 undated skipped" on a good day, and on any other day the name of each missing source and why — failed, timed out, or parsed nothing. It is shown when everything is fine on purpose: an indicator that only appears on failure teaches the reader not to look, and a line that reads the same every good morning is what makes a bad one legible at a glance.

Behind the line are distinctions the product keeps rather than blurs. A source that parsed zero entries is broken; a source with zero entries inside its window is merely quiet — they are counted separately and only the first is a failure. A disabled source is counted as disabled, never as missing. A card with no date is counted and named, never kept, because an identity made up from the fetch time would show it again every day. And an item the model failed to summarise stays on the page unsummarised, with the day marked partial: it has already been remembered, so dropping it would lose it for good.

The line is also checked from outside. A completeness check re-reads the raw bytes each source served, with a parser written independently of the product's own, and puts every entry into exactly one bucket — on the page, held back as already shown, outside the window, or unaccounted. On its first run, 2,401 entries: zero unaccounted, and the checker was shown to fire on three deliberately planted drops before its clean result was believed. The honest limit is stated with it: every check compares the product with what the sources served. A story a source never put in its own feed is invisible to all of it.

The categorisation failure is kept as a failure. The errors are concentrated in two pairs — tools read as products, and business news read as writing — and merging either pair would lift agreement to 91.4%. I kept six categories anyway: the taxonomy is a judgement about what I want to read, the instrument itself moves several points between identical runs, and a merge chosen to pass a number is chosen for the wrong reason. The trigger to revisit is a symptom I can notice while reading — a business story sitting under Writing often enough to annoy — rather than a statistic.

Four mornings of real use before anything was measured

The plan was to freeze the prototype and measure it straight away. One morning of actual use overturned that: the first reading, on 24 September, turned up ten findings — a date header that showed today above yesterday's news, titles that had silently broken, no sign of progress during a 44-second fetch that felt like one to two minutes, rankings mixed in among the news, two categories I could not tell apart. A freeze, it turned out, is a claim that the thing is finished enough to measure, and that claim had not been tested. So the stage was split: first use it every morning and fix what the use shows the same day; only after a morning that needs no change, freeze.

The second morning showed only five new items, and the product could now say why rather than leave me guessing: every source had answered, 80 items were inside their windows, and 75 of them had already been shown. It also showed that the rankings — 36 rows to five news items — had become most of the page, which prompted a proper source survey: 36 new sources were probed and added the same day, bringing the total to 48. The weekend mornings that followed brought one news item each, all others correctly held back, and no finding that needed a code change. The last fixes landed on the evening of 27 September; the measured mornings began the next day.

By then the prototype carried 261 automated tests, including a tripwire that fails any test which touches the real data. On the first day it caught one: a test whose leftover background work had written two lines into the real log.

Third attempt, and the method it produced

This is my third go at the same idea, and the first two failed in opposite directions. The first followed a plan step by step with Claude Code implementing each step; it produced a real, working codebase, but as it grew my review went shallow, and I was confirming that code existed rather than understanding it — I lost control of where it was going. The second applied strict rules to everything: probe before building, every design fork verified against real data, one decision per change, a complete written record. Control never slipped, and in three days it produced no demo — the rules were right, and they were spent on throwaway code. The lesson I wrote down afterwards is one line:

"Rigor has a phase."

Where it's thin

It is a prototype, and it runs on my machine. There is no scheduler — I press Fetch, by decision — no deployment and no second user. The production rebuild is where the code gets written to last; the code on GitHub today is the prototype.

The categorisation missed its bar. 83.1% against 85%, kept deliberately, with the two merges that would fix it already measured and waiting for a reason from real reading.

The completeness check has a blind spot it names. Five of the page readers are not checked independently — an independent parser would only duplicate them — so the check lists their items by name instead of calling them clean.

The same story from two sources is still two items. It has happened once, when Anthropic's newsroom and research pages carried the same post. The direction for when it matters is not hiding the copy but combining them — one entry per story, showing what each source says — which needs grouping of events across sources that the product does not do yet.

The breakfast-read and layout hypotheses have no answer yet. Their verdicts arrive after the fifth measured morning, and a morning that needs a code change restarts the count.
