What this project is

Diffusion models were, at the end of 2023, the engine behind almost every image generator people had actually heard of — and the papers describing them are dense with variational inference machinery that most engineering write-ups skip past. This project does the opposite: it takes the single hardest part, the derivation of the training objective, and works through it in full, including the two appendix derivations that the original DDPM paper leaves as exercises.

The deliverables were a 14-page report and a conference-style talk. The claim being defended is not a new model — it is that the deceptively simple DDPM loss, ‖ε − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖², is a consequence of maximum likelihood and nothing else, and that every simplification along the way is earned rather than assumed.

Why this is on an engineering portfolio. There is no product here and barely any code. What it demonstrates is the thing that is hardest to fake: the ability to take a research paper apart, find the load-bearing step, and rebuild the argument so someone else can follow it. The interactive figure above is the same skill pointed at a different medium.

The derivation in eight steps

Each step below states its result. Where the algebra is the interesting part, the full working from the report is one click away — skim the results, or read the whole thing.

Corrupt the data on a fixed schedule

The forward process adds a little Gaussian noise at each of T steps. It has no learned parameters at all — the variances βt are chosen in advance.

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right)$$

The √(1−βt) scaling on the mean is what keeps the variance from exploding as steps compound. For large T and a sane schedule, $\mathbf{x}_T \approx \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$.

Learn to walk it backwards

Generation is the reverse process. We can't compute it, so we approximate each reverse step with a neural network.

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\right)$$

Ask for maximum likelihood — and fail

The honest objective is to maximise the likelihood of the data. But p(x0) requires marginalising every intermediate noised state, which is intractable. So we maximise a lower bound instead.

$$\log p(\mathbf{x}_0) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)}\!\left[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)}\right] \;=\; \text{ELBO}$$

Split the bound into three interpretable terms

This is the pivot of the whole argument. The ELBO decomposes into a reconstruction term, a prior-matching term with no parameters, and a sum of per-timestep denoising terms.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{ELBO} \;=\; &\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{x}_1)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\;\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T\mid\mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{\text{prior matching}} \\[6pt] &-\;\sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\big)\big]}_{\text{denoising matching}} \end{aligned}$$

Show the full ELBO derivation

Expand the joint as a product over the Markov chain, peel off the t = 1 factor, and rewrite each remaining forward transition with Bayes' rule as $q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_0)$ — legitimate because the chain is Markov and conditioning on x0 adds nothing:

$$\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)} = \log\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_T)p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0\mid\mathbf{x}_1)}{q(\mathbf{x}_1\mid\mathbf{x}_0)} + \log\prod_{t=2}^{T}\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\mathbf{x}_0)}$$

Applying Bayes to the denominator turns the product into a telescoping series: all the intermediate $q(\mathbf{x}_{t}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)$ factors cancel, leaving $q(\mathbf{x}_1\mid\mathbf{x}_0)/q(\mathbf{x}_T\mid\mathbf{x}_0)$, which cancels the stray first-step term. Grouping what survives by expectation gives the three terms above.

Full working: Appendix A of the report.

Jump to any timestep in closed form

Compounding t Gaussian steps would be ruinous during training. The reparameterisation trick collapses all of them into one.

$$\begin{aligned} &\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t,\qquad \bar{\alpha}_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s \\[6pt] &q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\mathbf{I}\right) \end{aligned}$$

Equivalently $\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$. This one line is what makes training cheap — and it is exactly what the interactive figure at the top of this page evaluates.

Show why the steps collapse

Write $\mathbf{x}_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}$ and substitute recursively. At each substitution two independent Gaussians are summed; their variances add, so $\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-2}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{t-1}$ is a single Gaussian with variance $1-\alpha_t\alpha_{t-1}$. Repeating down to x0 leaves $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_0$.

Recover the true denoising step with Bayes

Conditioned on x0, the reverse step the network is chasing is available in closed form — and it is Gaussian.

$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\ \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\mathbf{x}_t+\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}(1-\alpha_t)\mathbf{x}_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t},\ \frac{(1-\alpha_t)(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{I}\right)$$

Crucially the variance depends only on the fixed schedule — not on the data and not on θ. That is what makes the next step collapse.

Show the Bayes derivation

$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)=\dfrac{q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1})\,q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_0)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0)}$, where all three factors are known Gaussians from steps 1 and 5. Multiplying the exponentials and collecting terms in xt−1 gives a quadratic — so the result is Gaussian, and completing the square reads off the mean and variance above.

Full working: Appendix B of the report.

Collapse the KL divergence to a distance between means

Give the learned reverse step the same (fixed) variance as the true one. The general KL formula for two Gaussians then loses every term but one.

$$\arg\min_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q\,\|\,p_\theta\big) = \arg\min_\theta \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta-\boldsymbol{\mu}_q\right\rVert_2^2$$

The log-determinant ratio becomes log 1 = 0 and the trace term cancels against −d. Training a diffusion model is now just mean regression.

Re-express the means — and the model becomes a noise predictor

Substituting x0 out of μq using step 5, and giving μθ the same shape, makes every fixed quantity cancel.

$$\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{\mu}_q &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_0 \\[6pt] \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta &=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\mathbf{x}_t-\frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t) \end{aligned}$$

$$\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{L}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\right\rVert_2^2$$

Nine steps of variational inference, and the network's entire job is to look at a noisy input and guess which noise was added.

What that means in code

The pseudocode in the report, written out as the training loop it describes. Every line maps back to a numbered step above. 

alphas_bar = torch.cumprod(1.0 - betas, dim=0)        # step 5

for x0 in loader:
    t   = torch.randint(0, T, (x0.size(0),))          # step 4: one term of the sum
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)                        # step 1: the noise we will hide

    ab  = alphas_bar[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
    xt  = ab.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps      # step 5: jump straight to time t

    loss = F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)              # step 8: guess the noise back

    loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

Four lines of substance. The distance between that and the derivation above is the whole point of the report.

A note on schedules

βt is a free choice, and it matters more than it looks. Ho et al. used a linear ramp from 1e−4 to 0.02. Nichol and Dhariwal later showed that this destroys information too early: by roughly two-thirds of the way through the chain the sample is already indistinguishable from noise, so the last third of the timesteps teach the model almost nothing. Their cosine schedule spends far more of the budget in the regime where the signal is actually being lost.

Toggle the two schedules in the figure at the top of this page and watch the image panel — the difference is visible around t ≈ 400.
