Can a teacher that never saw ageing teach a student to handle it?

Faces change across decades, and general face-recognition models trained on same-age photographs degrade badly when asked to match a person across twenty years. We distilled a strong general model — ElasticFace, ResNet-100, trained on ten million MS1M images — into students trained on the age-labelled B3FD dataset, on Compute Canada V100s in 48-hour jobs. The distillation transferred a 19-point gain on AgeDB into a weak student. Against the specialist model trained without a teacher, it came out roughly even. Both findings are on this page.

Every configuration, on every test set

Face-verification accuracy from Tables I–IV of the paper. The vertical mark on each bar is the weakest standalone model — ElasticFace trained on B3FD alone — and Δ is measured against it.

Read AgeDB first — it's the age-invariance benchmark. The original ElasticFace, trained on MS1M, sits at 98.3% and is untouchable. Trained on B3FD alone, the same architecture gets 57.0%. Distil the MS1M model into it and it climbs to 76.5%; distil MTLFace into it and it reaches 79.7%. MTLFace itself, built for exactly this task, gets 77.4% with no teacher at all.

The problem, and the datasets

A face-recognition model produces a feature vector per image; two images match if their vectors are close. Models trained on MS1M — ten million scraped images of a hundred thousand people, mostly at one age each — learn features that are superb at telling people apart and poor at recognising the same person twenty years on. The photographs that matter in missing-persons cases and border control are usually the old ones.

Four datasets did four jobs. MS1MV2 is where the teacher's knowledge comes from. B3FD — 375,592 images of 53,759 people, biometrically filtered from IMDB-WIKI and CACD and age-labelled from 0 to 100 — is the training set for every student. AgeDB (16,488 images, manually verified age labels) and CASIA-WebFace (494,414 images) were held out entirely and used only to test generalisation to people the models had never seen.

On the application. Age-invariant recognition is useful for finding missing children and verifying identity documents, and equally useful for surveillance without consent. The paper says this in its introduction and I'd rather it be visible here than buried: this is a capability with two edges, and building it comes with an obligation to say so.

OursKD: the distillation

The teacher is ElasticFace on a ResNet-100 backbone, pretrained on MS1MV2 by Boutros et al. — a 261 MB checkpoint that never updates. The student is either a second ElasticFace or MTLFace, the multi-task age-invariant model of Huang et al., trained on B3FD. Every image goes through both; the student is pulled toward the teacher by the loss.

The loss, as trained

Four terms, summed with equal weight. This is the exact criterion from the training script for the AdaTransLearn_ElasticTriL2 configuration: 

def criterion(out, labels, ages):
    s_features, t_features = out[0], out[1]     # student / teacher feature maps
    s_logits,   t_logits   = out[2], out[3]

    l2_loss  = mse(s_features, t_features)                                  # match the teacher's features
    kd_loss  = kd(s_logits / T, softmax(t_logits / T, dim=1))               # soft targets, T = 40
    ce_loss  = ce(s_logits / T2, labels)                                    # still learn the real labels
    tri_loss = triplet(s_features, labels, miner(s_features, labels))       # pull same identity together

    return l2_loss + kd_loss + ce_loss + tri_loss

The L2 term is the paper's core idea — "the teacher's answer to the question x is the feature map, and we want the student's answer to be as similar as possible." The cross-entropy term is the guard against the student merely copying a teacher whose features may not be right for this task. The soft-target and triplet terms are standard distillation and metric-learning additions.

Training at scale

None of this runs on a laptop. Each configuration is a SLURM job on Compute Canada: one V100, 32 GB of memory, a 48-hour wall-clock limit, B3FD untarred into node-local scratch at job start. The repository's cc_*.sh scripts encode every experiment as a reproducible job — model class, loss name, learning rate 2e-4, batch 64, temperature 40 — and the results doc traces every row in the tables above back to one of them.

What "verification accuracy" measures

Classification accuracy on training identities says little about recognising a stranger. So the models are scored on pairs: two images, one bit of ground truth — same person or not — and a decision made by thresholding the Euclidean distance between their feature maps. The threshold is chosen to maximise accuracy, because setting it to zero is useless: the same person at 20 and 45 will never produce identical features.

With no age gap the two distributions barely touch and the best threshold gets nearly everything right. At decades of gap, no threshold is good: move it right and strangers get accepted, move it left and the same person gets rejected. That overlap is the quantity an age-invariant model is trying to shrink — and it's what the AgeDB column measures.

What the numbers say

Two things, and they pull in different directions.

Distillation transferred generalisation. ElasticFace trained on B3FD alone reached 57.0% on AgeDB and 68.0% on CASIA. The same architecture with the MS1M teacher reached 76.5% and 82.1% — gains of 19.5 and 14.1 points on datasets it never trained on. The teacher pretrained on B3FD instead of MS1M gave far less (64.7% / 71.8%), which isolates the cause: what transferred was the diversity of the teacher's data, not the teacher's architecture. The paper's other observation points the same way — gains were largest when student and teacher differed, in structure or in pretraining.

Distillation did not clearly beat the specialist. MTLFace, trained on B3FD with no teacher, scored 96.3% / 77.4% / 82.2% across the three test sets. The best distilled student — ElasticFace with MTLFace as its teacher — scored 94.3% / 79.7% / 82.9%: ahead by 2.3 points on AgeDB, ahead by 0.7 on CASIA, behind by 2.0 on the B3FD test split. These are single training runs with no error bars, so the paper calls that "no significant improvement," and that's the right call. Measured against its cost — a second forward pass through a 100-layer teacher on every step — distillation as a compression strategy did not pay for itself here. The paper says so in as many words: "an indicator of the overall failure of our KD models."

The diagnosis, which is the useful part. Four loss terms at equal weight is a guess, not a design. The paper names it: the weights on L2, soft-target, cross-entropy and triplet losses were never tuned, and for MTLFace as the student — which has its own multi-task losses — they may have been pulling against each other. The baseline ElasticFace-on-B3FD was also never tuned, so the largest "gain" is partly a weak baseline. Neither is a mystery; both are budget.
